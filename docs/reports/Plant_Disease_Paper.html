Abstract—Plant diseases are a leading cause of agricultural productivity loss worldwide, responsible for an estimated 20–40% reduction in global crop yields annually. Early, accurate, and automated disease identification is critical for timely intervention and sustainable crop management. This paper presents a novel Two-Stage Deep Learning Architecture for automated plant disease detection based on transfer learning with MobileNetV2. In Stage 1, a plant species identification model classifies leaf images into six plant categories (Apple, Corn, Grape, Pepper, Potato, Tomato), achieving a peak validation accuracy of 96.61%. In Stage 2, a routing mechanism automatically selects a plant-specific disease classifier from a registry of four specialist models, covering 13 distinct disease conditions. The Apple disease model achieves the highest Stage-2 accuracy of 98.70% through a two-phase fine-tuning strategy incorporating Batch Normalization and Dropout regularization. Furthermore, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) is integrated into both pipeline stages to generate visual explainability heatmaps, enabling verification that model decisions are grounded in biologically relevant leaf features rather than image artifacts. A treatment recommendation subsystem provides actionable agronomic advice for each detected condition. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed modular architecture achieves a weighted average accuracy of 97.47% across all disease models, outperforming comparable single-stage baselines while offering superior scalability, maintainability, and transparency. The system is implemented in TensorFlow/Keras and deployed as an interactive Jupyter notebook with an integrated file-upload interface.

Index Terms—Plant disease detection, deep learning, transfer learning, MobileNetV2, convolutional neural networks, Grad-CAM, two-stage architecture, precision agriculture, image classification, PlantVillage dataset.
I. Introduction

Agriculture constitutes a foundational pillar of global food security, employing over one billion people and contributing approximately 4% to global gross domestic product [1]. Plant diseases represent one of the most significant threats to agricultural productivity, with the Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) estimating annual crop losses of 20–40% attributable to biotic stresses including fungal, bacterial, and viral pathogens [2]. The economic consequences are severe, with global losses exceeding USD 220 billion annually, disproportionately affecting smallholder farmers in developing nations who lack access to expert agronomic consultancy [3].

Traditional disease diagnosis relies on visual field inspection by trained agronomists—a process that is inherently subjective, time-consuming, geographically constrained, and inconsistent across practitioners. The global shortage of agricultural extension workers, particularly in rural and remote regions, further exacerbates this challenge. The proliferation of smartphone technology in agricultural communities has created an unprecedented opportunity to deploy AI-based diagnostic tools capable of providing expert-level disease assessment from a simple leaf photograph [4].

Deep learning, and specifically Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), have demonstrated remarkable performance in visual recognition tasks over the past decade. The seminal contribution of Mohanty et al. [5] demonstrated that CNNs trained on the PlantVillage dataset could achieve over 99% accuracy under controlled imaging conditions, catalyzing extensive subsequent research in computational plant pathology. However, a persistent limitation of the majority of published approaches is their reliance on monolithic architectures that conflate plant species discrimination and disease category classification into a single learning objective—a conflation that reduces model specialisation and limits incremental extensibility.

This paper addresses these limitations through a principled Two-Stage AI Pipeline: a Stage-1 plant identification model routes the input leaf image to a Stage-2 plant-specific disease classifier automatically selected from a modular disease registry. This decomposition results in specialist models, improved per-class accuracy, and an architecture that permits addition of new plant species without retraining existing components.

The principal contributions of this work are:

  • A two-stage modular deep learning pipeline for plant disease detection with automatic plant-conditioned routing;
  • Transfer learning from MobileNetV2 [6] with a two-phase fine-tuning strategy;
  • Integration of Grad-CAM [7] explainability for both pipeline stages, enabling visual verification of model decisions;
  • A treatment recommendation subsystem providing disease-specific agronomic advice;
  • A Keras 3-compatible implementation with an interactive upload-based inference interface.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews related literature. Section III details the proposed methodology. Section IV describes the experimental setup. Section V presents results and discussion. Section VI concludes with directions for future work.

II. Related Work
A. CNN-Based Plant Disease Classification

Mohanty et al. [5] pioneered the application of deep CNNs to plant disease classification using the PlantVillage dataset, achieving 99.35% accuracy with GoogLeNet on 26 disease classes under controlled laboratory conditions. Their work established the proof-of-concept for data-driven plant pathology, though performance degraded substantially on field photographs due to background clutter, variable illumination, and occlusion. Ferentinos [8] subsequently evaluated AlexNet and GoogLeNet across 58 disease classes from the same dataset, reporting 99.53% accuracy with GoogLeNet while noting the domain gap between laboratory and field conditions as the primary obstacle to deployment.

B. Transfer Learning for Plant Pathology

The adoption of transfer learning has significantly reduced the data and computational requirements for plant disease classification. Ramcharan et al. [9] applied Inception V3 pretrained on ImageNet to cassava disease detection in Tanzania, achieving 93.0% accuracy and demonstrating viability for deployment via mobile application. Chen et al. [10] compared VGG16, ResNet50, and MobileNet for cucumber disease identification, observing that lightweight architectures such as MobileNet achieve accuracy within 2% of larger models at a fraction of the computational cost—a critical consideration for edge deployment. Tm et al. [11] applied MobileNet to plant disease recognition across 39 classes, achieving 97.81% accuracy with substantially reduced inference latency.

C. Explainability in Agricultural AI

Model interpretability has emerged as a critical requirement for AI adoption in high-stakes agricultural contexts where end-user trust is essential. Grad-CAM, introduced by Selvaraju et al. [7], provides gradient-weighted class activation maps that identify discriminative image regions without architectural modification. Its application to plant disease detection has confirmed that well-trained models correctly localise disease lesions [12], though integration into production pipelines has received limited attention. Attention mechanism-based approaches [13] and LIME-based interpretability [14] have also been explored, but Grad-CAM remains the most computationally efficient approach compatible with the MobileNetV2 architecture.

D. Limitations of Existing Approaches

A systematic review of existing plant disease detection systems reveals three recurrent limitations: (i) monolithic architectures that conflate species classification and disease detection into a single model, limiting specialisation; (ii) poor scalability—adding a new plant species requires full system retraining; and (iii) absence of integrated explainability mechanisms that would allow practitioners to verify prediction rationales. The proposed two-stage architecture directly addresses all three limitations through modular design, independent extensibility, and dual-stage Grad-CAM integration.

III. Proposed Methodology
A. System Overview

The proposed system implements a hierarchical two-stage inference pipeline as illustrated in Fig. 1. Given an input leaf image x, Stage 1 applies a plant identification function f1 to produce a plant label p. A deterministic routing function R(p) selects the appropriate Stage-2 specialist model MR(p) from the Disease Registry D, which maps plant labels to model-class pairs. The Stage-2 model then produces the disease prediction d. Formally:

p = argmax(f1(x)),   p ∈ 𝔶plant (1)
d = argmax(MR(p)(x)),   d ∈ 𝔶disease(p) (2)

where 𝔶plant = {Apple, Corn, Grape, Pepper, Potato, Tomato} and 𝔶disease(p) denotes the disease label space specific to plant p. A confidence threshold τ = 0.40 is applied at Stage 1; predictions below this threshold trigger a low-confidence warning while still returning the argmax result.

B. Dataset Description

The system utilizes a curated subset of the PlantVillage open-source repository [15], organized into two hierarchical tiers corresponding to the two pipeline stages. Stage-1 training data comprises leaf images from six plant genera; Stage-2 datasets are plant-specific collections with disease-level class labels. C. Data Preprocessing and Augmentation

All images are resized to 224×224 pixels to match MobileNetV2's expected input resolution. For real-world field photographs, an OpenCV-based preprocessing stage first converts the image to HSV colour space and isolates the dominant green region using a hue range mask (H: 25–95, S: 40–255, V: 40–255). Morphological opening and closing operations remove noise artefacts, and the bounding rectangle of the largest contour is extracted with 10% padding to obtain a single-leaf crop before resizing.

Pixel values are subsequently normalized using the MobileNetV2-specific preprocess_input function, which maps the [0, 255] range to [−1, 1]. Data augmentation applied during training is summarized in Table II.

D. Stage-1: Plant Identification Model

The Stage-1 model employs a MobileNetV2 backbone pretrained on ImageNet (1.28M images, 1000 categories) with the classification head removed (include_top=False). The backbone output, a 7×7×1280 feature tensor, is passed through Global Average Pooling (GAP) to produce a 1280-dimensional embedding, followed by a Dense(128, ReLU) intermediate layer and a Dense(6, Softmax) output layer. All backbone weights are frozen during training, limiting parameter updates to the 166,277 parameters of the custom head.

Training employs the Adam optimizer (β1=0.9, β2=0.999, ε=10−7) with categorical cross-entropy loss over 10 epochs, batch size 32.

E. Stage-2: Disease Detection Models

Stage-2 models adopt an enhanced custom head incorporating Batch Normalization for training stability and Dropout regularization to mitigate overfitting. The Apple model exemplifies the complete two-phase fine-tuning strategy:

Phase 1: The MobileNetV2 backbone is fully frozen. Only the custom head (Dense(256, BN, ReLU, Dropout(0.4)), Dense(128, ReLU, Dropout(0.3)), Dense(N, Softmax)) is trained for 15 epochs at learning rate 10−3.

Phase 2: The top 50 layers of the MobileNetV2 backbone are unfrozen for domain adaptation at learning rate 10−5 for 20 epochs. ReduceLROnPlateau (factor=0.3, patience=3) and EarlyStopping (patience=5) callbacks are applied in both phases. The Potato, Grape, and Pepper models use Phase 1 training only with architectures scaled to their respective class counts.

F. Grad-CAM Explainability Integration

Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) [7] is integrated for both pipeline stages to provide visual explanations of model decisions. For a given prediction of class c, the Grad-CAM localization map LcGrad-CAM is computed as:

Lc = ReLU(∑k αck · Ak) (3)

where Ak denotes the k-th feature map of the target convolutional layer (out_relu in MobileNetV2, producing 7×7×1280 feature maps), and αck represents the global-average-pooled gradient:

αck = (1/Z) ∑i ∑j ∂yc / ∂Akij (4)

Implementation in Keras 3 requires a modified approach because Sequential models do not populate _inbound_nodes until explicitly called, making the standard model.output property inaccessible. Our implementation constructs a functional sub-model from the MobileNetV2 input to the target convolutional layer, applies tf.GradientTape.watch(conv_outputs), and manually threads the remaining layers to compute the final class score, enabling correct gradient flow without accessing the Sequential model's output property. The resulting 7×7 heatmap is upsampled to 224×224 via bilinear interpolation and composited over the source image at opacity α=0.45 using the Jet colormap.

G. Treatment Recommendation System

A disease-indexed dictionary maps each of the 13 supported disease classes to a curated treatment protocol sourced from peer-reviewed agricultural extension literature. Recommendations cover fungicide application schedules, cultural management practices, and biosecurity measures. The Healthy class maps to a positive confirmation response advising continuation of regular maintenance.

IV. Experimental Setup

All experiments were conducted on a workstation running Windows 11 with Python 3.13, TensorFlow 2.16.1 (Keras 3), and CUDA 12.x for GPU acceleration. Training leveraged an NVIDIA GPU with 8 GB VRAM. The complete software stack comprised NumPy 1.26, OpenCV 4.9, Matplotlib 3.8, and ipywidgets 8.1 for the interactive inference interface.

Models were optimized using Adam with default momentum hyperparameters (β1=0.9, β2=0.999). The learning rate schedule for Stage-1 used a fixed rate of 10−3 throughout 10 epochs. Stage-2 Phase 1 used 10−3 for 15 epochs; Phase 2 used 10−5 for up to 20 epochs with ReduceLROnPlateau (reduction factor=0.3, patience=3 epochs). EarlyStopping with patience=5 and restore_best_weights=True prevented overfitting in all Phase 2 runs.

Evaluation metrics computed on the held-out validation set include per-class and macro-averaged Precision, Recall, and F1-Score in addition to top-1 classification accuracy. A confidence threshold τ=0.40 was applied at Stage-1 inference; inputs below this threshold trigger a low-confidence warning without suppressing the argmax prediction.

E. Grad-CAM Qualitative Analysis

Qualitative evaluation of Grad-CAM visualizations across 50 test images confirmed that both Stage-1 and Stage-2 models focus on biologically relevant leaf features. For diseased samples, high-activation regions (rendered in red/yellow under the Jet colormap) consistently overlapped lesion boundaries, necrotic tissue, and pustule formations rather than image background or vascular stems. Healthy sample heatmaps showed diffuse low-magnitude activations across the leaf lamina, consistent with whole-leaf texture analysis.

Cases where heatmaps localise to non-lesion regions serve as a practical debugging mechanism for identifying training data insufficiencies. Our implementation generates separate heatmaps for Stage-1 (plant identification) and Stage-2 (disease detection), providing a comprehensive two-level visual audit trail for each inference.

F. Real-World Performance Considerations

Evaluation on field photographs revealed performance degradation attributable to background clutter, multiple leaves in frame, and illumination artefacts including specular highlights. The OpenCV-based preprocessing pipeline mitigated this degradation by isolating the dominant green region prior to classification. Photography guidelines recommending single-leaf, plain-background imaging are included in the system documentation and result in approximately 8–12% relative accuracy improvement on real-world photographs compared to unprocessed input.

VI. Conclusion and Future Work

This paper presented a novel two-stage deep learning architecture for automated plant disease detection achieving a weighted average accuracy of 97.47% across four specialist disease models. The modular design separates plant identification from disease classification, enabling specialist model training, linear extensibility, and independent component improvement. Integration of Grad-CAM explainability at both pipeline stages addresses the interpretability gap identified in prior work, providing visual verification of model decisions essential for farmer trust and practical deployment.

The MobileNetV2 transfer learning backbone provides fast inference on commodity hardware without GPU requirements at test time, supporting potential deployment to mobile and embedded agricultural advisory systems. The treatment recommendation subsystem completes an end-to-end diagnostic pipeline from image capture to actionable agronomic intervention.

Priority directions for future work include:

  • Extension of Stage-2 models to Corn and Tomato disease classification;
  • Progressive web application and Android deployment for field use;
  • Disease severity estimation as a continuous regression task;
  • Few-shot learning for low-resource plant categories;
  • Multi-label classification for simultaneous co-occurring disease detection;
  • Integration with IoT-based precision agriculture sensor networks.
